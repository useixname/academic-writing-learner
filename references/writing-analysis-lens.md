# Writing analysis lens

Use this reference when extracting lessons from papers or judging a revision. It preserves the original skill's central idea: learn the communicative decision behind an expression, rather than collecting impressive-looking language.

## Analyze at the appropriate level

| Level | Inspect | Learn only when useful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Word and collocation** | Technical terms, reporting/comparison/result/limitation verbs, ordinary academic wording, natural word pairings, alternatives, and claim strength. | The word's meaning, subject/object patterns, local function, section fit, and distinction from alternatives. |
| **Sentence** | Information order, syntactic structure, transition, emphasis, hedging, and relation to adjacent sentences. | A functional frame, not the source sentence. |
| **Paragraph** | Paragraph job, each sentence's role, progression, lexical cohesion, and bridge to the next paragraph. | The sequence of rhetorical functions when supported by the full paragraph. |
| **Section and paper argument** | Section-specific habits, argument progression, treatment of contribution, evidence, result, limitation, and implication. | A high-level organizational strategy, never a presumed template. |

Analyze only levels for which enough context is actually available. An isolated sentence can support a word, phrase, or sentence observation, but ordinarily cannot establish paragraph or section strategy.

## Rhetorical and cohesion lens

Identify the local purpose before judging wording. Relevant purposes include establishing background or importance; acknowledging progress; defining a problem; locating a gap; explaining why a limitation matters; introducing a solution; motivating a design or experiment; reporting, comparing, or interpreting results; explaining an observation; acknowledging a limitation; and drawing an implication or conclusion.

For sentence-to-sentence flow, inspect whether the text uses controlled terminology, reference words, repeated concepts, contrast, cause, consequence, continuation, elaboration, or a problem-response relation. Prefer coherent prose over a sequence of separately grammatical sentences.

For information ordering, test whether the reader receives a useful progression such as known to new information, observation to interpretation, problem to consequence to response, or claim to evidence to implication. Change ordering only when it materially improves the paragraph's job and the revision budget permits it.

## Section-aware expectations

Do not transplant expressions mechanically across sections. As a heuristic, introductions often carry motivation, prior progress, gaps, limitations, and contributions; methods carry definitions, operations, design choices, and procedure; results carry measurements, comparisons, and observations; discussion carries interpretation, possible explanations, implications, and limitations. These are lenses for observation, not mandatory templates.

## Language calibration

Apply this priority within a sentence:

```text
precision > clarity > domain conventionality > stylistic sophistication
```

Before proposing an uncommon, formal, or more elaborate word, ask:

1. Does it carry a meaning or technical distinction the existing word lacks?
2. Is it conventional in the relevant supplied literature or required by a user-defined term?
3. Does it fit the sentence's rhetorical role and claim strength?
4. Is a simpler alternative equally precise?
5. Does it create vocabulary drift from the surrounding manuscript or the user's natural register?

If an ordinary word is equally precise and natural, keep it. Do not make purely cosmetic substitutions such as `use` to `utilize`, `improve` to `ameliorate`, or `show` to `demonstrate`. Conversely, do not replace a genuine domain term merely because it is uncommon.

Treat words sometimes associated with generic AI prose—such as *delve*, *pivotal*, *multifaceted*, *intricate*, *holistic*, *seamlessly*, *leverage*, *notably*, or *remarkably*—as context-dependent, not forbidden. Use them only when they are precise, locally natural, and justified by the evidence or user text.

## Claim strength, hedging, and emphasis

Interpret claim verbs in context. A rough orientation is that `demonstrate` and `establish` are often stronger than `show` or `indicate`, while `suggest`, `appear to`, `may`, and `might` often express more cautious interpretation. The words themselves do not determine evidentiary strength; the study design, wording around them, and user-provided evidence do.

Introduce hedging only to match a real uncertainty, distinguish observation from interpretation, or accurately delimit scope. Do not add it merely to sound academic. Likewise, require evidence before adding intensifiers such as `substantially`, `consistently`, `significantly`, `notably`, or `remarkably`.

## Learning value filter

Prioritize patterns that teach a decision a researcher can reuse: framing a gap, motivating an experiment, reporting or interpreting results, handling comparisons and trade-offs, discussing limitations, or moving between ideas. Ignore routine text unless it illustrates such a decision.

For every retained pattern, explicitly record its `appropriate_when` and `avoid_when` conditions. A phrase is not a lesson until its functional conditions are known.
